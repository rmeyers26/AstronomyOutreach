What Is a Galaxy?

A galaxy is a massive system of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter, all held together by gravity. Galaxies range from tiny dwarf galaxies with just a few million stars to enormous giants with trillions — and everything in between.

Our home galaxy, the Milky Way, is a barred spiral galaxy about 100,000 light-years wide. The Sun sits roughly 26,000 light-years from the centre, out in one of the galaxy's spiral arms — a quiet suburb of a very large cosmic city.

Galaxies are not scattered randomly. They cluster together in groups and superclusters, connected by filaments of gas and dark matter in what astronomers call the "cosmic web." The Milky Way belongs to a group of about 50 galaxies called the Local Group.

Key Concepts Explained

Middle School
🌌

Galaxies — Cosmic Cities

A galaxy is held together entirely by gravity. Nothing else — no walls, no glue — just the mutual pull of billions of stars, plus enormous amounts of invisible dark matter that we can't see but know is there because of how galaxies rotate and bend light.

Analogies

  • 🏙️City of stars: Think of a galaxy as a city, and stars as the buildings. The Milky Way is a city of 250 billion buildings. You live in a building about halfway from the city centre, in a quiet neighbourhood called the Orion Arm.
  • 🌀Spinning pizza dough: Spiral galaxies look like spinning discs because they rotate — the same way pizza dough spreads outward when spun. The "dough" here is made of billions of stars and gas clouds.
  • 🍿Popcorn bowl: The universe has galaxies clustered together in "bowls" (galaxy clusters) with empty voids between them — like popcorn clumped in some parts of a bowl and missing in others.
🌀

Spiral Galaxies

Spiral galaxies — like the Milky Way and Andromeda — have a bright central bulge and sweeping arms of stars, gas, and dust. The arms are actually density waves, not fixed structures — stars drift in and out of the arms over millions of years, like traffic bunching on a highway.

Analogies

  • 🚗Traffic jam: Spiral arms aren't like the spokes of a wheel. Stars slow down when they enter a denser region — like cars bunching up near an on-ramp — then speed up again when they leave. The arm is the "jam," not the cars.
  • 🌊Ocean wave: A wave moves across the ocean, but the water molecules mostly stay in place, just bobbing up and down. Spiral arms are waves of density moving through a galaxy — the stars bob, but the wave travels.
  • 🎆Pinwheel firework: The spiralling shape looks like a pinwheel sparkler — but instead of sparks lasting a second, these "sparks" are stars that live for billions of years.
🥚

Elliptical Galaxies

Elliptical galaxies are smooth, featureless blobs of mostly old, red stars. They contain very little gas or dust, so almost no new stars are forming inside them. Many giant ellipticals likely formed from the merger of two or more spiral galaxies — including the far-future fate of the Milky Way and Andromeda.

Analogies

  • 🏚️Old neighbourhood: Elliptical galaxies are like old cities where no new buildings are being built — all the residents (stars) are retired, and nothing new is happening. Quiet, old, massive.
  • 🍳Scrambled eggs: When two spiral galaxies merge, the structure gets scrambled — like stirring two bowls of differently-coloured scrambled eggs together until the swirls disappear. The result is a featureless blob.
  • 📦Storage unit: Unlike spirals buzzing with star formation, ellipticals are like packed storage units — full of old stuff (old stars), nothing new coming in.
⭐

The Milky Way — Our Galaxy

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy — it has a central bar of stars from which the spiral arms extend. At its centre lurks a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*, about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. We orbit this centre once every ~225 million years — one "cosmic year."

Analogies

  • 🌏Living inside a dinner plate: We can't see the full shape of the Milky Way directly because we're inside it — like trying to describe the shape of a dinner plate you're sitting on. We map it by studying star distances and radio waves.
  • 🕐The cosmic clock: Our solar system has been around ~4.6 billion years and has completed about 20 full orbits of the Milky Way. That's like saying Earth is "20 galactic years old."
  • 🌆Suburb of a giant city: Our Sun is 26,000 light-years from the galactic centre — living in the quiet outskirts, far from the chaotic, radiation-blasted downtown where Sagittarius A* lives.
🌑

Dark Matter

Galaxies spin too fast at their edges — based on visible mass alone, the outer stars should fly off into space. They don't. Something invisible holds them in. We call it dark matter: it makes up about 27% of the universe's total content, but emits no light and has never been directly detected. We know it exists through its gravity alone.

Analogies

  • 🎪Invisible tent poles: Imagine a circus tent held up by invisible poles you can't see or touch — you only know they're there because the tent doesn't collapse. Dark matter is the invisible structure holding galaxies together.
  • ⚖️Missing weight: If you put a galaxy on a cosmic scale, the stars and gas only explain about 15% of the weight you measure. The missing 85% is dark matter — you can't see what's tipping the scale.
  • 🌬️Wind in a storm: You can't see the wind, but you can see trees bending and debris flying — you infer the wind's strength from its effects. We infer dark matter from how its gravity bends light and spins galaxies.
💥

Galaxies in Collision

Galaxies collide — and when they do, it takes billions of years. The Milky Way and Andromeda are currently approaching each other at about 110 km/s and will collide in roughly 4.5 billion years. Despite the name, very few stars actually crash into each other — the space between stars is so vast that they mostly pass right by.

Analogies

  • 👻Two ghosts walking through each other: Stars inside galaxies are so far apart that two colliding galaxies pass through each other like two swarms of fireflies — the fireflies rarely collide directly, but the groups get mixed together.
  • 🥊Slow-motion punch: A galactic collision in fast-forward would look like a dramatic explosion — but in real time it unfolds over billions of years, slower than the human mind can grasp. It's a punch delivered in super-slow-motion.
  • 🔭Nature's trigger: Collisions compress gas clouds and trigger enormous waves of new star formation. A galaxy merger isn't pure destruction — it's also a cosmic nursery, birthing millions of new stars.

Famous Galaxies — Close Neighbours & Showpieces

🌌

Andromeda · M31

Nearest Large Galaxy

The Andromeda Galaxy is the most distant object visible to the naked eye — a faint smudge in the constellation Andromeda. It's a barred spiral larger than the Milky Way, containing ~1 trillion stars. It's on a direct collision course with us.

🔭 Naked-eye visible from dark skies. In ~4.5 billion years, it merges with the Milky Way to form "Milkomeda."

☁️

Large Magellanic Cloud

Satellite Galaxy of the Milky Way

A dwarf irregular galaxy visible from the Southern Hemisphere as a detached piece of the Milky Way. About 160,000 light-years away, it's one of the Milky Way's satellite galaxies — slowly being shredded by our gravity over billions of years.

🌟 Home to the Tarantula Nebula — the most active star-forming region in our local neighbourhood.

🌀

Whirlpool Galaxy · M51

Classic Interacting Spiral

The Whirlpool is a grand-design spiral galaxy about 23 million light-years away, shown actively merging with its smaller companion NGC 5195. It was the first galaxy in which spiral structure was identified, by Lord Rosse in 1845.

🔭 Visible with binoculars near the end of the Big Dipper's handle. The merging companion makes it a textbook galaxy collision.

🎩

Sombrero Galaxy · M104

Edge-on Spiral with Huge Bulge

Seen nearly edge-on, the Sombrero's bright core and wide dust lane give it the appearance of a hat. It's about 28 million light-years away and hosts a supermassive black hole roughly a billion times the mass of the Sun — one of the most massive known in nearby galaxies.

🔭 Visible in a small telescope in Virgo. The dark dust lane is one of the most dramatic features in the night sky.

Mind-Blowing Galaxy Facts

🔢

More Galaxies Than Grains of Sand

The observable universe contains an estimated 2 trillion galaxies. Each galaxy averages hundreds of billions of stars. That means there are roughly 10 times more stars in the observable universe than grains of sand on every beach and desert on Earth — combined.

👁️

You Can See Another Galaxy Naked-Eye

On a clear, dark night you can see the Andromeda Galaxy — 2.537 million light-years away — without any equipment. The light entering your eye left that galaxy before modern humans existed. You're literally seeing the ancient past with your own eyes.

💨

The Milky Way Is Moving — Fast

Our solar system orbits the Milky Way's centre at about 828,000 km/h (514,000 mph). The Milky Way itself hurtles through space toward the "Great Attractor" at ~2.2 million km/h. You are never sitting still — you are always hurtling through the cosmos at staggering speed.

🕳️

Every Large Galaxy Has a Giant Black Hole

At the centre of almost every large galaxy — including the Milky Way — sits a supermassive black hole. In 2022, astronomers captured the first image of ours: Sagittarius A*, with a mass of 4 million Suns. Despite its fearsome size, it's currently quiet — not actively feeding.
