Overview

The Celestron CPC 1100 is a flagship consumer Schmidt-Cassegrain telescope, combining an 11-inch (280 mm) aperture with an integrated GPS-enabled computerised mount. It is built for serious amateur astronomers who demand high resolution, portability, and automated alignment.

Its dual-fork alt-azimuth mount and NexStar+ hand controller give observers access to a database of over 40,000 celestial objects — from planetary detail to faint deep-sky galaxies — with one-touch GoTo slewing.

Core Technologies

🛰️

GPS Auto-Location

Built-in GPS acquires your exact location and time at power-on, removing the need to enter these manually. You still perform a brief alignment (SkyAlign uses any three bright objects; Auto 2-Star is another option) — but the GPS eliminates the guesswork and speeds up the process significantly.

🌀

StarBright XLT Coatings

Celestron's multi-layer optical coatings push light transmission to over 97.4% per surface. The real-world benefit: improved contrast and light throughput on low-contrast targets — galaxy cores, subtle planetary banding, and faint nebulosity — not just raw brightness.

📡

NexStar+ Hand Controller

Database-driven GoTo with 40,000+ objects and multiple alignment modes (SkyAlign, Auto 2-Star, EQ North with wedge). Add the optional WiFi adapter and the scope integrates with SkySafari or the Celestron app for full smartphone control and planetarium overlays.

🔭

Schmidt-Cassegrain Optics

The SCT design folds a very long focal length into a compact tube using a corrector plate (front), primary mirror, and convex secondary. Strengths: long focal length, high image scale, excellent for planets, globulars, and small galaxies. Limitation: narrow native field of view — large objects like open clusters need a focal reducer to fit comfortably.

📸

Astrophotography — With Nuance

In standard alt-az mode: excellent for planets and short exposures. For long-exposure deep-sky imaging you need an equatorial wedge, careful polar alignment, and ideally autoguiding — without a wedge, field rotation limits exposures to roughly 1–2 minutes. A 0.63× focal reducer (sold separately) converts the system to f/6.3 for a wider field and faster imaging.

⚡

AllStar Polar Alignment

Celestron's AllStar routine provides a streamlined polar alignment procedure directly from the hand controller — no polar scope needed. Note: this only applies when using an equatorial wedge. In standard alt-az visual use, polar alignment is not required or performed.

Ideal Observing Targets

  • Planets — Saturn: Cassini Division, banding; Jupiter: belts, GRS, moons
  • Moon — crater detail down to ~1 km scale in good seeing
  • Globular clusters — M13, M5: begins resolving individual stars
  • Planetary nebulae — Ring Nebula (M57), Dumbbell (M27): disc and structure visible
  • Galaxies — Andromeda core, Virgo Cluster members (dark skies required)
  • Double stars — sub-arcsecond splits in excellent seeing

Who It's For

Best suited for observers ready to move beyond 6–8" class scopes, those focused on planetary and high-resolution visual work, and users comfortable managing a heavier setup (~24 kg / 53 lbs).

Less ideal if you want grab-and-go convenience, prioritise wide-field views without accessories, or are just starting out. It transitions into deep-sky imaging with a wedge and reducer — but that is a separate investment in equipment and technique. Expect a learning curve — but a profoundly rewarding one.

Understanding the Optics

🪣

Aperture

280 mm · 11 inches

What does it do?

Aperture determines two things: light gathering (how faint an object you can see) and resolution (how fine the detail). Compared to a 4" (100 mm) scope, the CPC 1100 gathers roughly 7.5× more light. That's what starts resolving individual stars in globulars and turning planetary smudges into structured detail.

Everyday analogies

🪣 Rain bucket: A wide bucket collects more rainwater than a narrow cup in the same storm. More aperture = more light collected from the same patch of sky.
👁️ Your pupils: In a dark room, your pupils open wide to let in more light. The CPC 1100's 11" mirror is like having a pupil the size of a dinner plate.
🔦 Flashlights: A wide flashlight beam lights up more area than a narrow one. More aperture = more sky "lit up" for your eye to see.
🪐 Saturn comparison A cheap 4" telescope shows Saturn as a blurry oval with rings. The CPC 1100's 11" mirror reveals the Cassini Division — a real gap between Saturn's rings — plus dark cloud belts on the planet itself. That's what more aperture buys you.
📏

Focal Length

2,800 mm (110 in)

What does it do?

Focal length sets the image scale — how large an object appears at the focal plane. It does not determine magnification by itself; magnification depends on both the telescope focal length and the eyepiece you choose. Longer focal length means a larger image scale and a narrower field of view at any given eyepiece.

Everyday analogies

🛋️ TV distance: Sitting 2 feet from a TV makes it look huge. Sitting 20 feet away makes it look tiny. Focal length is like that distance — longer = bigger image.
📷 Camera zoom lens: When you slide a zoom lens out, the barrel gets longer — that's increasing the focal length. The photo zooms in. Same idea here.
🎯 Hallway trick: Draw a tiny dot on the far wall of a very long hallway. From down the hall it looks bigger than it does up close. More focal length = more magnification of tiny objects far away.
The magnification formula:
Magnification = Telescope FL ÷ Eyepiece FL
2,800 ÷ 25 mm = 112×
2,800 ÷ 10 mm = 280×
Practical limit is ~300–400×, set by atmospheric seeing — not the optics. On most nights 200–250× is the ceiling for crisp images.
⚗️

Focal Ratio

f/10

What does it do?

Focal ratio = focal length ÷ aperture. For the CPC 1100: 2,800 ÷ 280 = f/10. An f/10 system is considered "slow" — it has a narrow, high-magnification field and requires longer exposures for imaging. Add a 0.63× focal reducer and the system becomes ~f/6.3: wider field, faster for imaging, better suited to extended objects like nebulae. For visual planetary work, f/10 is ideal.

Everyday analogies

🧃 Juice funnel: A wide, short funnel pours juice fast (f/4). A narrow, tall funnel pours slowly and in a thin stream (f/10). "Fast" scopes gather light quickly; "slow" scopes zoom in tighter.
🚿 Shower head: Wide spray = covers a big area, low pressure (fast ratio). Narrow jet = covers a small area, high pressure (slow ratio). Same water, different focus.
📸 Phone camera: Portrait mode (blurry background, tight zoom) is like f/10. Landscape mode (wide, everything sharp) is like f/4. The CPC 1100 is built for "portrait mode" of planets.
f/4 — Fast · Wide f/10 ← CPC 1100 f/15 — Slow · Narrow

Practical Performance Notes

🌡️

Thermal Equilibration

Allow 45–90 minutes for the OTA to cool to ambient temperature before serious observing. Thermal currents inside a warm tube create a shimmering effect that destroys fine planetary detail and limits effective magnification — especially noticeable above 200×. Plan accordingly: set up first, align, then wait before pushing magnification.

🎯

Collimation

SCTs require occasional collimation — adjusting the secondary mirror alignment so the optical axis is correct. Miscollimation is most obvious at high magnification as asymmetric star images or a soft, off-axis planetary disc. Check collimation on a bright star before planetary sessions. The process takes 5–10 minutes and only the secondary adjustment screws are involved.

🔍

Field of View Reality

At f/10 the native field of view is relatively narrow. Large targets — Pleiades, Orion Nebula in full, large galaxies — won't fit well without a focal reducer. The CPC 1100 is optimised for high-magnification work on compact targets: planets, planetary nebulae, globulars, double stars. For wide-field views, add the 0.63× reducer or use a separate wide-field instrument.
